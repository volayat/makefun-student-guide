## 1. What is make.fun?

[make.fun](https://make.fun) is a platform where people post **bounties** — small, clearly described challenges with a prize attached — and other people complete them to win the prize.

Think of it like this:

- **Someone posts a bounty:** "Build a small game that does X", "Write a guide that explains Y", "Make a tool that solves Z". The bounty page states the prize, the deadline, and exactly what counts as a finished entry.
- **You build an entry:** you make the thing, following the rules on the bounty page. Most entries are code, a guide, a design, or a small app.
- **Entries are checked and ranked:** depending on the bounty, entries are scored automatically, judged, or ranked by a measurable result (for example, how many verified signups a guide brings in).
- **Winners get paid:** prizes are usually paid in USDC on Solana. You claim a prize with a Solana wallet address.

That is the whole model. There is no fee to browse bounties, no fee to enter the free ones, and no requirement to spend money to take part. The platform makes money from the people posting bounties, not from charging students to enter.

### What make.fun is not

Clearing this up early saves you disappointment:

- **It is not a job.** There is no salary, no guaranteed income, and no boss assigning you work. You choose what to enter, and you only get paid if your entry wins or earns a reward.
- **It is not passive income.** Every prize comes from work you actually did. If a bounty looks like "do nothing and earn", read the rules twice — it almost certainly means something else.
- **It is not gambling.** You are not betting money on outcomes. Free entries cost you time, not cash. Never pay to enter a bounty unless you have read the rules, understood the cost, and decided it is worth it — and as a student, the default in this guide is simple: **stick to free entries**.
- **It is not a shortcut past learning.** The bounties that pay best are the ones that need real skill. That is actually good news for a student: the work doubles as practice.

### Why students do well here (when they do)

Students have three advantages that are easy to miss:

1. **You are already learning.** A bounty that asks for a small web app is homework with a prize attached. The same hours teach you the same skill either way.
2. **You have fragmented time.** Many bounties are built for short, focused sessions. A 45-minute gap between lectures is enough to make real progress if the task is sliced small.
3. **You have a network.** Classmates, study groups, campus clubs, and student chats are full of real people. If a bounty rewards bringing in genuine new users — as this one does — a student who shares honestly with real friends has a real advantage over someone shouting into the void online.

The disadvantage is just as real: your time is limited, and exam season will crush any routine that is not designed to bend. Sections 6–8 of this guide are built around that fact.

## 3. Your first week: a gentle on-ramp

Do not try to win in week one. Try to learn the loop. The loop is: **read the rules → build a small entry → submit → see what happened → note what to do differently.**

### Day 1–2: Observe

- Browse the open bounties. Do not enter yet.
- For each bounty that interests you, write one line in your notebook: what it asks for, the deadline, and the prize.
- Notice which bounties get many entries and which get few. A bounty with fewer entries is often a better first target than a famous one with hundreds.

### Day 3–4: Pick one small bounty

- Choose the smallest free bounty you genuinely understand. "Small" means you can describe the finished entry in one sentence.
- Read its rules a second time and write down the exact deliverables: file names, size limits, what must be in the entry, and the submission steps.
- Set up the workspace: a folder for the entry, the tools you need, and a blank checklist of the deliverables.

### Day 5–6: Build in two short sessions

- Session 1 (30–45 minutes): build the roughest version that satisfies the rules. Ugly is fine. Complete beats perfect.
- Session 2 (30–45 minutes): fix the obvious gaps, check every rule against your entry, and submit.
- If you run out of time, submit what you have rather than missing the deadline — a finished small entry teaches you more than a perfect unfinished one.

### Day 7: Review

- Look at your entry with fresh eyes. What would you change?
- If results are out, read them honestly. Did you misunderstand a rule? Was the bar higher than you expected? Write the answer down — that note is worth more than the entry itself.
- Decide one thing to improve next time, and only one. Improving everything at once is how students burn out.

**A realistic first-week goal:** one submitted entry, one page of notes, and a clear idea of how long a bounty actually takes you. That is a win, even if the entry does not place.

## 4. Free-only bounties: earning with zero capital

This is the core of the student strategy. Free-only means you never pay an entry fee, never buy tokens to take part, and never need paid tools. Your only investment is time — so you must spend that time where it is most likely to pay off.

### What "free" covers — and what it does not

- **Free:** browsing, entering, building with free tools, submitting, and (if you win) claiming a prize to your wallet.
- **Not free:** transaction fees when you claim a prize (a tiny amount of SOL), any paid software you choose to use, and your time. The SOL fee is small, but know it exists so it never surprises you.
- **Never pay for:** entry fees to a bounty you have not researched, "premium" access sold by strangers, or anyone offering to enter for you. Those are the three most common ways students lose money around bounty platforms. Before you enter, also check the prize claim window on the bounty page — a prize you forget to claim in time is a prize you never receive.

### How to find the free bounties worth your time

Filter every bounty through these four questions before you commit an evening to it:

1. **Is entry actually free?** Read the bounty page and the rules. If there is a cost, skip it — at least while you are starting out.
2. **Can I finish it in the time I have?** Be honest about your week. A bounty with a 3-day deadline during term time is a different commitment from one with a 3-week deadline.
3. **Do I understand what "good" looks like?** If you cannot picture a winning entry, you will guess — and guessing wastes your scarcest resource.
4. **Will I keep the skill afterwards?** A bounty that teaches you something you can reuse (a framework, a writing skill, a tool) pays twice: once if you win, once on your CV.

### The maths of free entries (be honest with yourself)

Suppose a bounty pays $1,000 split across 50 places, and 500 people enter. Your chance of placing is not 10% — the field is not evenly skilled, and a careful entry from someone who read the rules beats dozens of rushed ones. But even so, most entries will not win. That is normal, and it is why the strategy in this guide is:

- **Enter several small free bounties** rather than betting everything on one big one.
- **Reuse your learning** — each entry should leave you faster and better at the next one.
- **Count the skill as part of the pay.** If a bounty teaches you a tool you later use in coursework or a job application, that value stays even when the prize does not come.

If you need guaranteed money this month, make.fun is the wrong tool — get a part-time shift instead. If you can afford to invest a few hours a week in work that might pay and definitely teaches, it is a good fit.

### Free tools that cover almost every bounty

You do not need paid software. This short list covers most student entries:

- **Code:** a free editor (for example, VS Code) and a free GitHub account.
- **Writing and guides:** any markdown editor, or just GitHub's own editor.
- **Design:** free tools such as Figma (free tier) or Canva (free tier) for simple graphics.
- **Video:** your phone camera and a free editor, if a bounty asks for a short demo video.
- **Research:** the bounty page itself, the platform's own documentation, and public sources linked from it. Start from the official material, not from random summaries.

If a bounty seems to require a paid tool, stop and re-read the rules. Usually there is a free way to meet them — and if there genuinely is not, that bounty is not a free-only bounty, so skip it.

## 5. Picking bounties that teach you real skills

The highest-value choice a student makes is not which bounty pays most. It is which bounty leaves you with a skill you can prove.

### Skills employers ask about that bounties teach

| Skill | Bounty types that build it | CV proof you keep |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Building small web apps | App and game bounties | A live link + GitHub repository |
| Writing clear technical guides | Guide and documentation bounties | A published guide people actually used |
| Working to a specification | Any bounty with strict rules | An entry that passed every stated check |
| Shipping on a deadline | Any bounty with a short window | A submitted entry with a timestamp |
| Explaining ideas to strangers | Referral and guide bounties | A guide with real readers or signups |
| Researching before building | Bounties with a research step | Notes showing what you checked first |

### A simple picking framework

Score each bounty you consider from 1 to 5 on these three questions, and enter the highest total:

1. **Can I finish it?** (time, deadline, tools — be realistic)
2. **Will I learn something reusable?** (a skill, a tool, a pattern)
3. **Is the prize reachable?** (number of paid places, size of the field, clarity of the scoring)

A bounty that scores 5-5-3 beats one that scores 3-2-5. The prize is uncertain; the learning is not. Over a semester, the student who always picks 5-5-3 ends up with a portfolio and a set of skills — and, because careful entries place more often, usually with more prizes too.

### Match the bounty to your degree (when you can)

- **Computer science / engineering:** app, game, and tool bounties. Your coursework already teaches the pieces.
- **Design / media:** guide, branding, and visual bounties. Your portfolio grows directly.
- **Business / marketing:** referral and growth bounties, like this one. You practise distribution, which is a real job skill.
- **Writing / humanities:** guide and explainer bounties. Clear writing is a scarce skill in technical fields, and it shows.
- **Any degree:** a guide bounty in your own field. Explaining your subject to newcomers is one of the best ways to learn it deeply — and a good guide is genuinely useful to other students.

You do not have to match your degree. But when a bounty overlaps with what you are already studying, the same hours count twice, and that is the most efficient earning a student can do.

## 6. Fitting bounty work between classes

Your timetable is the constraint, so design around it instead of fighting it.

### The 30–60 minute block

Most student free time is not a free afternoon — it is a gap. Design every bounty task to fit one gap:

- **30 minutes:** read a bounty page fully, or fix one named problem in your entry, or write one section of a guide.
- **45 minutes:** build a rough first version of a small feature, or draft a whole short guide section.
- **60 minutes:** a full build-and-check cycle for a small entry, or a careful revision pass against the rules.

Before each block, write down the one thing you will finish in it. "Work on my bounty" is not a task — it is a wish. "Write the getting-started section and check it against the rules" is a task, and you will know when it is done.

### A template week (adjust the days to your timetable)

| Day | Block | Task |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | 45 min after class | Read one bounty page; decide enter or skip |
| Tuesday | 30 min gap | Set up the entry folder; list exact deliverables |
| Wednesday | 60 min evening | Build the rough version |
| Thursday | 30 min gap | Fix gaps; check every rule against the entry |
| Friday | 45 min evening | Revise, polish, submit |
| Saturday | 60 min (optional) | Start the next bounty, or rest — rest is allowed |
| Sunday | 30 min | Review the week; write one page of notes |

That is about four hours in a normal week. It is enough for one small entry a week, which is a serious pace over a semester. It is also small enough to survive a busy week: if you miss Wednesday, you can still submit something simpler on Friday.

### Three rules that keep the routine alive

1. **Never let a bounty eat a lecture.** Missing class to chase a prize that might not come is bad maths. The degree is the bigger prize.
2. **Stop at the end of the block.** A timer that ends your session protects the next one. Tired work is slow work, and it creates bugs you will spend the next block fixing.
3. **Keep a "next step" note.** At the end of every block, write the single next step for the following block. Future-you will start in seconds instead of spending twenty minutes remembering where you were.

### When your timetable is genuinely full

Some weeks have no spare blocks. That is fine — skip the week. A bounty routine that demands a perfect week will break in week three. One that survives a missed week will still be running in week ten, and the student who is still entering in week ten is the one who eventually places.

## 7. Exam weeks: how to pause without losing everything

Exam weeks are not a failure of discipline. They are the point of being a student. Plan for them the way you plan for a deadline: in advance, on purpose.

### Two weeks before exams

- Finish or submit any bounty you are in the middle of. A half-finished entry hanging over you during revision is a distraction, not an asset.
- Do not start a new bounty with a deadline inside exam week. No matter how good the prize looks, the timing is wrong.
- Write a one-line handover note for each open entry: where it stands, what is left, and whether it is worth returning to after exams.

### During exam week

- **Pause bounty work entirely.** This is the rule, not the exception. Your revision time is worth more than any single bounty prize.
- If a bounty you care about has a deadline during exams, let it go. There will be another bounty. There will not be another sitting of that exam.
- The only bounty-related thing worth doing in exam week is nothing. If you catch yourself "just checking" the leaderboard, close the tab and go back to revision.

### The week after exams

- Give yourself one rest day before restarting. You will work better for it.
- Re-read your handover notes. Pick the most promising unfinished entry and either finish it in one focused block or drop it cleanly. Dropping it is a decision, not a failure — it frees the slot for something better.
- Restart with the smallest possible bounty, not the biggest. Momentum matters more than ambition after a break.

### What pausing does not cost you

- Your account, your referral code, and your past entries stay where they are. Nothing expires because you took two weeks off.
- Your referral link keeps working while you revise: if someone signs up through **https://make.fun/r/71xfynpg** during your exam week, that still counts. You do not have to be online for your link to work.
- Your skills do not decay in two weeks. You will be slightly rusty for one session, then back to normal.

## 8. A sample semester plan

This is a plan for a typical 14-week semester. Treat it as a template: move the blocks to fit your real timetable, your real exam dates, and your real energy.

### Weeks 1–2: Learn the platform

- Finish the getting-started checklist in Section 2.
- Submit one small entry, following the first-week plan in Section 3.
- Goal: one submitted entry, one page of notes, no expectations about winning.

### Weeks 3–6: Build the habit

- One small entry per week, using the template week in Section 6.
- After each entry, write down one thing you would do differently.
- Start sharing your referral link honestly (see Section 9) — with friends who might genuinely like the platform, not with strangers.
- Goal: four entries, a visible improvement between the first and the fourth, and a small portfolio starting to form.

### Weeks 7–9: Go deeper once

- Pick one bounty that is bigger than your usual — one with a longer deadline and a larger prize — and give it two weeks instead of one.
- Use the extra time for the parts students usually skip: reading past winners, checking your entry against every rule, and revising once after a night's sleep.
- Goal: one careful entry you are genuinely proud to show someone.

### Weeks 10–11: Exam preparation

- Wind down bounty work (see Section 7). Finish or park open entries.
- Do not start anything new with a deadline inside exam week.

### Weeks 12–13: Exams

- Pause. Revise. Sit your exams. That is the job.

### Week 14: Restart small

- One rest day, then one small bounty to rebuild momentum.
- Review the whole semester: which entries taught you most, which type of bounty suits you best, and what you want to try next semester.

### What a good semester looks like

At the end of 14 weeks, a realistic good outcome is:

- 6–8 submitted entries
- 2–3 portfolio pieces you would show an employer
- 1–2 skills you can now name and demonstrate (not just claim)
- A referral link that has brought in a few genuine signups
- Maybe a prize — and if not, a clear, evidence-based idea of what to improve next semester

That is a semester well spent. It is also, quietly, more than most students manage — because most students never start, or start big and quit in week three.

## 9. How referrals work (and what they do not do)

Referrals are the part of make.fun that students ask about most, and the part most often misunderstood. Here is the honest version.

### The basic idea

Every make.fun account has its own referral code. Mine is **`71xfynpg`**, and my link is **https://make.fun/r/71xfynpg**. When someone signs up through that link, their account is bound to my code, and I am credited when the platform's referral check verifies that signup.

You get your own code the same way: it is on the **Referrals** tab of your profile. Once you have it, your link is `https://make.fun/r/<your-code>`.

### What counts — and what does not

This matters, because the rules are strict for a good reason: they exist to stop fake signups.

- **Counts:** a real person who signs up through your link, creates their own account, and actually takes part on the platform.
- **Does not count:** your own second account, signups from people who share your identity or network, clusters of signups from one address, accounts that never do anything, and bots. The platform's referral check verifies each signup, and a signup it rejects never counts.
- **Timing matters:** a signup counts when the account is created inside the competition window with your code bound to it. Each signup also goes through a review hold (about 24 hours) before it is verified, so counts you see during a competition are provisional until the final check after the deadline.

The practical consequence is simple: **referrals reward bringing real humans, not generating signups.** Share your link with people who might genuinely want to use make.fun, and do not try to manufacture signups — it will not work, and it can get entries disqualified.

### Sharing your link on a student budget (free, honest, effective)

You do not need paid ads. You need real people who trust you. In rough order of effectiveness:

1. **Direct messages to friends who build things.** A personal message — "I found this platform where you can win prizes for small builds, I thought of you because of your project" — beats a broadcast every time. Only message people for whom it is genuinely relevant.
2. **Your study group or course chat.** If the group already talks about side projects, a short message with what make.fun is and your link is welcome. If the group never talks about that, do not be the person who spams it.
3. **A campus club or society.** Coding clubs, design societies, and entrepreneurship groups are full of people who already do this kind of work for fun. Offer to show them your entry, not just your link.
4. **Your own guide or post.** Write something useful first — a short post about what you built, or a guide like this one — and put your link inside it. People follow useful content; they ignore bare links.
5. **Your GitHub profile.** If you publish entries on GitHub, your profile README is a natural place for a short line about make.fun and your referral link.

### What not to do

- Do not post your link in every group chat, server, and comment section you can find. It annoys people, and it brings in low-quality signups that will not verify.
- Do not pay for signups, and do not offer to pay people to sign up. That is not a real referral, and the verification check will treat it that way.
- Do not create extra accounts for yourself or ask friends to create accounts they will never use. Each person gets one account, and fake accounts do not count.
- Do not mislead people about what make.fun is. Saying "free money, just sign up" will bring signups who never take part — which helps nobody, including you.

### A realistic expectation

A student who shares honestly with real friends might bring in a handful of verified signups over a semester. That is a good result, and it is worth more than a hundred unverified ones. In a referral bounty like this one — where the entry with the most verified signups wins a share of $1,000 across the top 50 — those handfuls add up across a whole cohort of students, but no single student should expect to top the board from referrals alone. The guide matters because it is useful; the referrals follow because it is shared by real people with real readers.

## 10. Turning bounty work into a CV

Bounty work only helps your career if you can show it. Keep proof from the start, because you will not remember the details in six months.

### Keep these four things for every entry

1. **The repository.** Every entry that lives on GitHub is a portfolio piece. Keep the repository public, tidy, and readable — a stranger should understand what it does in thirty seconds.
2. **A one-line description.** Write one sentence per entry: what it does, what it was for, and what you learned. That sentence is the raw material for your CV.
3. **A screenshot or link.** A live demo link is best; a screenshot is second best. Employers believe what they can see.
4. **The result.** Did it place? How many entries were there? What did the feedback say? Even "submitted, did not place, learned X" is useful — it shows you ship work and learn from it.

### CV lines you can honestly write

Translate each entry into a CV bullet using this shape: **what you built + what it did + what you used.**

- "Built a small web game for a make.fun bounty (JavaScript, HTML/CSS); submitted on deadline and ranked in the top half of 200+ entries."
- "Wrote a getting-started guide for make.fun as a public GitHub README; shared with classmates and brought in verified new users through my referral link."
- "Entered 6 free bounties in one semester, shipping one entry per week while studying full-time."

Notice what these lines do: they prove you can build, write, ship on a deadline, and manage your time. Those are the things employers actually screen for, and a bounty entry proves them better than a claim on its own.

### Your GitHub profile is your portfolio

- Pin your two or three best entries to your GitHub profile.
- Write a short profile README: who you are, what you are studying, what you build, and a link to your best work.
- Keep your repositories tidy: a clear README, a licence, and no leftover clutter. A hiring manager who opens a messy repository will not read further.

### Interview stories

Bounty work gives you honest interview stories, which are worth more than rehearsed answers:

- "I entered a bounty with a 3-day deadline during term time. I sliced the work into 45-minute blocks between lectures, shipped a complete entry, and it placed. Here is what I would do differently next time."
- That answer shows planning, time management, and self-awareness — and it is true, which makes it easy to tell.

## 11. Common student mistakes

These are the mistakes I see students make most often, including the ones I made myself.

1. **Entering the biggest bounty first.** The biggest prize attracts the strongest field. Start small, learn the loop, then go bigger.
2. **Skipping the rules.** Every refused entry I have heard about came from someone who did not read the rules fully. Read them twice: once before you start, once before you submit.
3. **Building before checking the deadline.** A brilliant entry submitted one hour late is worth nothing. Put the deadline in your calendar the moment you decide to enter.
4. **Chasing every bounty at once.** Three half-finished entries lose to one finished one. Pick one, finish it, then pick the next.
5. **Spending money to "get started".** You do not need paid tools, paid courses, or paid entry. If something asks you to pay before you have earned anything, walk away.
6. **Sharing your referral link everywhere.** See Section 9. Spam brings unverified signups and a bad reputation. Share with real people, personally, and only where it is welcome.
7. **Treating a loss as a waste.** An entry that does not place still taught you something and still belongs on your GitHub. The waste is entering, losing, and learning nothing because you never reviewed what happened.
8. **Letting bounties eat study time.** The degree comes first. A prize is a bonus; a failed exam is a cost that lasts far longer.

## 12. FAQ

**Do I need money to start on make.fun?**
No. Browsing and entering free bounties costs nothing. The only money that ever appears is a small amount of SOL for transaction fees if you claim a prize — and you only face that after you have already won.

**How much can a student realistically earn?**
Honestly: small amounts, irregularly. Some students win a prize in their first semester; many do not. Treat it as paid practice with upside, not as income you can budget around.

**I have never written code. Can I still enter?**
Yes — look for guide, writing, and explainer bounties first. Clear writing is a real skill, and guide bounties (like this one) are designed for people who can explain things well. You can learn basic coding alongside, one small bounty at a time.

**How much time does one bounty take?**
A small one: 3–5 hours spread across a week. A bigger one: 8–15 hours across two weeks. If a bounty looks like it needs more than that, it is probably not a good student pick — unless the deadline is long and the prize is large.

**What if I miss a deadline?**
You lose that entry, and that is all. Note what went wrong (started too late? underestimated the work?), and use the lesson on the next one. Missing a deadline is not a mark against your account.

**Can I enter with friends?**
You can collaborate and learn together, but each person has one account, and an entry is submitted under one account. Check the bounty rules for how teams are treated — and never share one account between people.

**Is my referral link still worth sharing if I only have a few friends who might join?**
Yes. A few verified signups from real people are worth more than many unverified ones. Quality beats quantity, and the verification check is designed to reward exactly that.

**What happens to my entries after a competition ends?**
They stay on your GitHub, in your portfolio, under your name. A guide like this one stays useful after the contest ends — that is part of the point.
